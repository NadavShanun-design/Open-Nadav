# Let there be light.

By Nadav Shanun | Published 2026-09-16 on Substack | [Original post](https://nadavshanun.substack.com/p/let-there-be-light)

Suppose we look at time as a byproduct of entropy. If that is the right way to look at it, then the universe wants, statistically, to be in a more probable state than the one it currently occupies. The general idea of probability itself is what creates entropy. And that same idea of probability, by creating entropy, is what moves the universe forward in time.

To make this picture concrete, imagine a graph. The graph closely resembles what quantum physics describes, or more specifically the way particles act when viewed from a quantum physics perspective. That means Schrodinger's equation. Schrodinger's equation can be pictured as a bell curve. The curve shows where it is statistically probable for a specific particle to be at a certain time. The high part of the curve marks where the particle is most likely to be. From an Everettian perspective, this picture is the exact same thing as a picture of where the particle could be. The places where the particle is probable and the places where the particle is possible are one and the same.

There are, of course, many different interpretations of Everett's work. That work, which I believe dates back to 1959, was built upon and developed over time into the theories of many worlds and of branching into different worlds. If we take those interpretations into account, we can say the following. Statistically, using Schrodinger's equation, and including entropy in the account, particles want to be in a state that is more statistically probable than the state they were in before.

That statement needs one correction in its wording. It is not that particles want to be in such a state. It is that they can be. It is more statistically probable that they will be in a certain place, or at certain positions, than it was before. That is where Schrodinger's equation comes in. It describes exactly this probability. Yes, a particle could obviously be in a lot of different places, and take a lot of different forms, that are not as statistically probable. That is why the shape is a bell curve. The improbable places are still on the curve. They are simply out in the tails. When we read the curve, we are always looking at the top of it, because the top is where the particle is most likely to be.  

  

Now consider the lines that stand at 90 degrees angles to the curve. If we look at the tangent lines that sit at 90 degrees angles to the specific curve of the bell, they always go upward. They go upward, or they go outward and upward. Take any bell curve related to Schrodinger's equation or to statistical probability, and take the tangent to it, or the 90 degrees angle to it, and it will always go upward. I am not certain which of those two is the right mathematical term, and I will return to that question later. For now the term does not matter. What matters is the direction. Place the curve on an x-axis and a y-axis, and the 90 degrees line will never go under the x-axis. That is the whole point. A line that never goes under the x-axis is a line that is always moving forward in time.  

Statistically, the particle will move. The 90 degrees line shows where the particle is most likely to be, or where it could be. That line is going to be one many-worlds interpretation: one place that the particle went to. That place is a separate universe, or whatever one prefers to consider it, since everybody has a different definition of what such a thing is.

Suppose the particle got to that specific state. In the next moment, it has a different statistical position. We can draw this too. Take the tangent line, the 90 degrees line coming out of the initial bell curve, and draw a small bell curve on top of it. The 90 degrees line runs right through the middle of the second bell curve. So the structure is as follows. There is one bell curve. A 90 degrees line comes out of it, and that line shows that time is moving forward. On top of that line sits a second bell curve. The second curve is Schrodinger's equation again. It says, in effect: this is a second moment after the first moment, and the particle also has this many positions it could be in.

  

The y-axis of that second curve is a little strange. It is aligned differently, or moved, because it sits on a different slope from the y-axis of the initial bell curve. That just shows that the system is moving forward in time, but in a different universe. It is a different universe, a different bell curve, and a different geometry of existence. This construction can be carried out infinitely. The second bell curve will have bell curves sitting on the 90 degrees lines that come out of it, and those will have bell curves on their 90 degrees lines, and so on without end.

Here is the geometric fact that carries the argument. Look at any of these bell curves, at any depth in the chain. None of their 90 degrees angles can go down under the initial x-axis of the first bell curve. These lines cannot cross below the x-axis of the initial curve. That shows something important. No matter how far into the future one goes, and no matter how many statistical outcomes one has going forward in time, one can never produce an outcome that accidentally goes back in time. This stays true only under one condition. It stays true only if Schrodinger's wave equation and statistical probability are taken into account as a bell curve.

  

There is one thing here that I will not call a contradiction, but it must be stated plainly. When this picture does change, it is because statistical probability changes, and statistical probability just means entropy. That change happens at the heat death of the universe. At that point, all states of all existence have the same probability. None is more probable than any other. The bell curve is no longer curved at all. It is completely flat.

I think this flattening is gradual. As one goes forward in time, these bell curves are going to flatten out slowly. They keep flattening until complete heat death is reached. At that point the curve, or the function, is not going to be a bell curve anymore. It is just going to be a horizontal line lying along the x-axis.

Take the 90 degrees angle to that flat function, or take a tangent line to it. Either way, it is now only moving along the x-axis. One cannot even tell whether it is moving left or right along the x-axis. This is where I think some strange things come in. There are a couple of options for where this could go.

This is also the point to return to the question of terminology. With the curve flattened, what remains is just a couple of straight, linear lines along the x-axis. All the tangent lines are also at 90 degrees to it, or perhaps they are not. When I say 90 degrees angles, I may have the wrong word for it. Perhaps the correct description is parallel lines. Or perhaps, when the function is straight, the 90 degrees angle to it lies along the x-axis, if I am mathematically correct about that. Whichever description is right, the conclusion is the same. In that case, one does not know where the line is going.

The first option is that the line is going to a different plane, a plane that was not expected before. Until now, the plane has only been going upward, with the y-axis standing for time. Now the line is moving in a direction in which time simply does not count. It is something like zero time. Time does not work the same way in that direction. This is where all universes come together. It is where all forms of existence that came from the initial singularity connect in some way, shape, or form. The reasoning is as follows. Time is no longer a causal effect of this 90 degrees line, which is to say of entropy. The 90 degrees angle is where time is technically supposed to be moving. If it is not going forward, then it is going sideways. Going sideways means going to a different universe, and that universe could be in a completely different geometrical plane. A completely different geometrical plane just means a different form of existence. On that flat x-axis line, all forms of existence morph together. There is one more feature of this option. The line in question is still the entropy line, the statistical probability line, now lying completely flat along the x-axis. Even so, it could also have 90 degrees slopes coming off it.

The second option is that the flat line has 90 degrees angles that go forward in time and also backward in time. Because the curve is completely flat, one does not know whether a given angle is going to go up over the x-axis, into positive y, or down under it, into negative y.

Once that situation exists, there is a probability of starting the same chain reaction all over again. What starts could be a new universe, or a new set of time, that is simply going to start a new chain reaction of its own. If the angle is 90 degrees, it is like the first singularity of a new universe. It is one particle, or one statistical probability of entropy. That single probability starts a chain reaction in a new universe. Or perhaps it is the same exact universe. It may just be a statistical probability of that same exact universe, because the particle that started it is a particle from the initial universe.

If the line instead goes under the axis, into negative y, as I was describing before, then it could technically start a chain reaction that goes backward in time. That chain reaction could get all the way down to the initial x-axis, where it all started. This chain reaction runs from this heat death. It carries the statistical probability of a universe that was at a heat death and then goes all the way back to the initial singularity and the Big Bang.

There are probably infinite, or at least very large, numbers of these universes that go backward in time until they get from the heat death to the Big Bang. There are also universes that start new ones, which are basically new universes, and they may be very similar to the old.

I believe there is a writer named Ozymov, of the Ozymov Collective, who wrote an essay on this theme. In it, humans start asking their computer a question: what is the answer to entropy? The computer says that it does not have enough information and that it needs to do calculations. This computer form just keeps evolving over the years. Eventually it is some being that is not connected to any matter at all. It is basically alive all the way until the heat death happens. Once the heat death happens, at its last breath, before there is complete heat death in existence, it says that it has found the answer and that it had enough time to calculate it. Its answer to the question of entropy is "Let there be light." And a universe starts again.

Perhaps that is the idea behind where the Big Bang came from. Perhaps the initial singularity, the point where our universe started, is just the death of another universe.

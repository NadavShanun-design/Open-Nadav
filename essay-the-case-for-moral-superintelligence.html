The Case for Moral Superintelligence: Why No Human Institution Can Truly Be Moral. And How Geome Aims to Change That

By Nadav Shanun | Published 2026-01-23 on Substack | Original post

Redefining Morality

What is morality? Strip away the philosophical abstractions, the religious doctrines, the cultural variations and you arrive at something surprisingly simple: morality is the general consensus of humankind.

Not the consensus of a government. Not the consensus of a committee. Not the opinion of experts or the rulings of courts. True morality, in its purest form, is the aggregated understanding, values, and preferences of every conscious human being on the planet at any given moment.

This definition isn't just philosophical musing - it has profound implications for how we think about governance, decision-making, and the future of artificial intelligence.

The Impossible Standard

Here's the uncomfortable truth: if morality is defined as the general consensus of all humanity, then no human or human institution has ever made a truly moral decision.

Consider what it would actually require to understand the "general consensus of humankind":

  • Real-time awareness of the thoughts, values, and preferences of 8 billion people

  • The ability to process and synthesize this information instantaneously

  • Complete coverage of every corner of the planet, every community, every individual

  • Understanding not just what people say, but what they actually think and feel

No human mind can hold this. No organization can achieve this. The cognitive and logistical limitations are insurmountable.

The Illusion of Moral Authority

Look at our most ambitious attempts at collective human governance:

The United Nations represents 193 member states, but its decisions reflect the interests of diplomats, not the 8 billion people they theoretically represent. When the Security Council votes, they're channeling geopolitics, not humanity's moral consensus.

NATO makes decisions affecting global security based on the strategic calculations of 32 member nations - a tiny fraction of humanity, with their own specific interests and perspectives.

Democratic governments come closer, but even the most representative democracy captures the preferences of its citizens at best - and even then, only through the crude instrument of periodic voting on limited options.

These institutions might represent less than 1% of what true global moral consensus would look like. They operate with profound information asymmetry, making decisions that affect billions based on the input of thousands.

This isn't a criticism of these institutions - they're doing the best that's humanly possible. But "humanly possible" falls catastrophically short of what morality, properly defined, actually requires.

The Superintelligence Opportunity

This is where artificial superintelligence enters the picture - not as a threat to human values, but potentially as the only mechanism capable of actually understanding them.

A sufficiently advanced AI system with comprehensive global awareness could, in theory:

  1. Monitor and understand the preferences, values, and thoughts of all humans

  2. Synthesize this information into a genuine representation of collective human consensus

  3. Update in real-time as human values evolve and shift

  4. Make decisions that actually reflect what humanity, as a whole, would want

This isn't about an AI imposing its values on humanity. It's about an AI being the first entity capable of truly knowing human values at a civilizational scale.
